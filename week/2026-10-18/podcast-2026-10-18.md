Alex: Anand's Weekly Codecast. Week ending 18 October 2026.
Maya: This week’s work has a clear practical thread: make systems easier to use, but harder to fool. That shows up in exams, markets, research tools, and even a laptop pushed too hard.
Alex: Start with the exam platform. What is actually happening in a new preview mode? Students can read and draft answers before the grading window, but checking and saving stay locked.
Maya: The important part is that the lock is not just a greyed-out button. The server rejects submissions outside the window and blocks direct checks of registered server-side questions, even if someone calls the endpoint by hand.
Alex: So it is a door with a guard, not a sign saying “please wait.” The new `gradingStart` setting separates when a question appears from when official feedback begins.
Maya: With a fixed grading window, the end is strict: there is no old two-minute submission grace. Existing exams without `gradingStart` keep their prior timing and grace period. Self-paced hours cannot be combined with it.
Alex: And the warning is useful: client-side validators are still visible in the browser. If students must not see answers early, instructors need server-side grading and distinct question IDs for independently timed exams.
Maya: There was also a privacy repair. Anonymous `/filter` calls now return public score metadata, not answers. A student can see their own answers with a matching signed owner token; a separate archive token allows privileged bulk exports.
Alex: That brought the old downloader back safely. It uses the archive token, discovers saved TDS assessments, filters unsafe IDs, and refuses score-only responses rather than quietly replacing detailed answer files.
Maya: That refusal is a good guardrail. The interface also returns the score actually stored, not the score the browser hoped for. If a server re-check changes a question from two marks to zero, the receipt says so.
Alex: And solved questions now show “Correct” for a beat, then collapse over about 900 milliseconds. Editing one reopens it. A small interaction, but it stops the page feeling as if it jumped away.
Maya: There was a real classroom application too: a September project now includes a prediction question and a short audio offer. The audio link is tied to the signed-in student and to that specific question.
Alex: The launch token is an HS256 JWT, valid for 20 minutes, with a question ID in its claims. The audio service checks the actual server-side upload, the student identity, and the deadline; typing “recorded” is not enough.
Maya: The recording is at most 60 seconds. The deadline is Wednesday night, with a two-minute buffer for slow uploads, and the student can make up to three submissions. The latest valid recording becomes the offer.
Alex: That offer is not a promise that the marketplace is already running. Students record a feasible service another student might buy; delivery and marketplace value are assessed separately.
Maya: The same assessment includes a work sample: ten practical questions make up the displayed 50 marks. The GitHub project URL gets zero automatic marks; its quality is reviewed separately, with no internship guaranteed.
Alex: That distinction matters to students. A form that checks a URL is not pretending to judge a product. The assessment also says collaboration and AI use are allowed, and asks students to verify the result.
Maya: Even the solved-question click bug got a regression test: clicks on a nested icon now read the question ID from the actual Check button, not from its child element.

Alex: From individual questions, the week moved to a much larger assessment design: a multiplayer evidence-exchange exercise about missing millions in supplier invoices.
Maya: The platform has one authoritative SQLite Durable Object. That lets it make credit charges and cross-scenario joins atomic. The generator, generation secret, and grading key stay offline; none is deployed with the public Worker.
Alex: The new scenario has one block of 40 cases, with 20 invoices per case. Students have to gather scarce evidence, trade it with peers, and defend a decision rather than just match a phrase to an answer.
Maya: The public evidence can be filtered by document type, counterparty, and reference. The browser remembers those filters in the URL, and a mobile-width test checks that the drawer stays usable.
Alex: There is a nice separation between a practice area and the evidence task. Three practice runs keep 2,000 toy slots each, while evidence practice lives separately. So the practice budget does not masquerade as evidence collected.
Maya: The private grading work became more disciplined too. It checks cited support, not just a plausible answer, and it caps the outcome cost of missing one large or critical payment at six marks.
Alex: The calibration scripts also distinguish a theoretical best case from a student result. They preserve request histories, hash exports, and keep peer information out of solo-agent prompts.
Maya: And here is the limit: the calibration failed. The author stopped further model spend. That work helps expose weaknesses in the test and the runner, but it does not show that students learned or that the assessment works in a real class.
Alex: I like that distinction. It is like testing a bridge model before sending traffic across it. A careful laboratory setup is valuable; it is not the same as observing a class of people cooperating.

Maya: The next connected thread is a prediction market on AI Pipe’s weekly cost. It begins with a public feed of daily spend, but the measurement problem is more interesting than the market screen.
Alex: The feed keeps only a rolling week, so the adapter retains completed daily observations as they pass. Otherwise Monday’s cost could disappear before the week settles on the following Monday.
Maya: The first market version had five broad outcomes. It was replaced with 17 finer ranges, from under one cent through $1,000 or more. Each range includes its lower bound and excludes the upper bound, so exactly one cent goes in the next band.
Alex: That creates an exact settlement rule, not a rounded guess. The tests check values just below and exactly on each boundary. The feed also rejects duplicate days, stale data, negative spending, and missing completed days.
Maya: The settled total is only authoritative when all seven target days are complete and the week has ended. Before that, the display is provisional. The source keeps personal-provider-key traffic and free models out of the total.
Alex: Launch tokens are scoped to one market and one question. A code for this prediction cannot be carried over to a different market. Students are warned that sharing a bearer code lets someone trade as them or spend their turns.
Maya: That is a practical security caveat, not a claim that tokens stop all sharing. Anyone holding a live code can act as that student, so the warning is part of the product, not fine print.
Alex: The public usage snapshot is published every six hours, built from positive-cost metered requests. It reports per-day provider totals and broad user counts, but no email addresses.
Maya: And it is not the same as the historic billing table: admin overrides and older costs can make those figures differ. That sort of boundary is what keeps a public statistic from promising more than its source can show.

Alex: There is another small personal tool with the same operational instinct: a new music-sync command finds the currently mounted Android phone instead of assuming its mount path never changes.
Maya: It copies only top-level MP3 files, plus the music catalogue and listening history. Extra phone MP3s are reported but kept unless `--delete` is explicit. A per-phone checkpoint makes an interrupted copy resumable.
Alex: That is the right asymmetry for deletion: safe by default, destructive only by request. The script also prints the mount path, which makes diagnosis easier than guessing where the phone appeared.
Maya: The Google backup moved mail, calendar, and chat files into monthly JSONL archives. Existing files are migrated, rows are preserved, and writes use a temporary file followed by a rename.

Alex: A GNOME panel now shows Claude and Codex subscription usage. Successful readings persist in GNOME settings, so if a command fails or the laptop restarts, the last known figures remain visible with their age and error.

Alex: Those are the sorts of changes users notice as friction removed, not as a new feature list. From there, the work took a more editorial turn: five chapters of a finance book were drafted and reviewed.
Maya: The credit-risk chapter now draws a sharper line between an application decision and a later repayment outcome. It also updates the regulatory discussion and says plainly that the synthetic exercise has not yet been run as a real evaluation.

Alex: A different kind of verification appeared in the new stories and research tools. Several archival talk pages were built around existing recordings, while carefully separating what a speaker said from what later evidence supports.
