Alex: Anand's Weekly Codecast, for the week ending 20 September 2026. This week has a strong theme: measure what a model does, then design the workflow around what the evidence can actually support.
Maya: And the clearest example is confidence. A model saying “I’m 95 percent sure” sounds useful, but that number only helps if it predicts when the answer is wrong.
Alex: Anand’s BANKING77 work compares stated confidence with token log probabilities. A log probability comes from the model’s own next-token scoring, rather than asking it to describe how sure it feels.
Maya: The clever part is making the prediction itself a single token. Each of 77 support-routing labels is mapped to a one-character code, and the model emits that code first. Its log probability can then be compared with its stated confidence on the same answer.
Alex: So it’s a cleaner comparison than asking two separate questions and hoping the model behaves the same way both times. On 770 cases, the raw log probabilities ranked correct answers above errors better, with an AUROC of 0.830 versus 0.783.
Maya: But ranking risk and giving a trustworthy probability are not the same job. The log probabilities had a worse raw Brier score: 0.148 against 0.119 for top-two stated confidence. A Brier score measures how far probability estimates are from the outcomes.
Alex: That distinction matters in practice. A score can sort cases from safer to riskier without meaning “this one has exactly a 73 percent chance of being correct.”
Maya: Then came the useful test: freeze the routing cutoffs on those 770 cases, and try them on 2,310 untouched BANKING77 requests. No adjusting the rule after seeing the new answers.
Alex: At the five-percent error target, the frozen log-probability rule let 65.2 percent of cases pass without review, with 4.38 percent observed error. Stated confidence passed 41.2 percent, with 2.63 percent error.
Maya: So the log-probability rule removed more review, but accepted more mistakes. It stayed within the chosen five-percent limit, though. That’s a trade-off, not a universal win.
Alex: And the holdout prevents a much bigger claim. The ten-percent log-probability cutoff produced 12 percent error, above its target. A rule that worked at one operating point did not transfer across the whole risk curve.
Maya: Also, calibrated Brier scores were nearly tied on the holdout, and their paired confidence interval crossed zero. The evidence supports better review coverage at the tested five-percent cutoff, not “log probabilities are always better confidence.”
Alex: I like that result because it ends with a usable boundary. Learn a threshold from historical cases, freeze it, then check that exact threshold on fresh data. Don’t turn one good point into a grand law.

Maya: There was a second, smaller benchmark beside it: Jev, a decision model that returns a choice rather than generated prose, classified one example from each of the 77 BANKING77 intents.
Alex: Jev got 58 of 77 right, or about 75 percent. DeepSeek V4.1 Flash reached 79 percent, and GPT-5.6 Luna reached 83 percent. Jev cost about seven cents per thousand classifications.
Maya: That’s cheap, but not dramatically cheaper than those alternatives: roughly eight cents for DeepSeek and twelve for Luna. The sample is only one frozen example per intent, so I wouldn’t treat those accuracy gaps as final rankings.
Alex: Right. Jev sits on the cost-quality frontier mainly because it’s inexpensive, not because it’s the strongest classifier. And the analysis flags a more specific limitation: its risk-at-five-percent statistic uses the same 77 cases to choose and assess the cutoff.
Maya: Which is why the separate holdout work is so valuable. It gives us a practical lesson in evaluation design: exploratory results can suggest a promising threshold, but they don’t establish that it will behave the same way on new cases.

Alex: That same appetite for evidence showed up in a much more playful experiment: asking newer language models to pick a number from zero to one hundred. The answer is not anything like a uniform draw.
Maya: GPT-4.1 Nano chose 57 two hundred times out of two hundred at temperature zero. Even at temperature one, it picked 57 in 78 cases, or 39 percent, and produced only 16 distinct numbers.
Alex: Temperature made the sample less repetitive, but didn’t make it random. GPT-5.6 Luna chose 47 in 108 of 200 runs and 73 in another 58; only six different numbers appeared.
Maya: That’s a useful distinction for anyone interpreting sampling settings. More variety is not the same as a fair draw. The newer experiment also preserves raw outputs, model and sampling details, provider routing, token use, and cost, not just the winning number.
Alex: And the script saves after each successful response, then skips work already recorded. That makes a 2,400-call experiment resumable rather than something you have to restart because a network request failed halfway through.
Maya: The model-comparison data got a routine refresh too. Nothing in the change proves a new price-quality result by itself, but keeping the model scores current is what lets a frontier chart remain useful instead of becoming an old screenshot.

Alex: From model measurements to publishing: Anand also built a deterministic path from a talks catalogue into blog posts. The important change was a rejection of having an AI invent prose for each entry.
Maya: Instead, a script reads structured talk details and writes the post in a fixed format. It can include the session date, event, location, summary, links, and selected images, so the same input produces the same post.
Alex: The catalogue is the source of truth. A `talk_url` in existing blog front matter marks a session as already covered, and the generator leaves existing files alone. That is a simple guard against both duplicate posts and overwriting edits.
Maya: It also limits generation to entries marked as latest, and the script reports how many posts it created, found already, or skipped. There are tests for dates, image labels, and how links are made absolute.
Alex: One operational detail: if a talk has no time, the generated date defaults to noon in India Standard Time. When a time is known, the configured offset is preserved. That’s a sensible default, but still a default someone should replace when better data exists.
Maya: And images are curated in the catalogue, rather than every slide being dumped into the post. The generator can include the talk’s main visual and useful links while keeping the entry predictable and editable by a human afterward.
Alex: The supporting workflow for making talk stories was tightened as well. It now says to inspect what’s actually in a talk folder, keep companion pages like demos or surveys when they matter, and patch an existing story instead of flattening it into a new template.
Maya: Then update the catalogue, not its generated list. That separation is a little like keeping the recipe in one place and treating the printed menu as an output. It reduces the chance that two versions drift apart.
Alex: The blog work also took a more careful approach to synthetic data. The revised guidance asks for realistic reference ranges, causal mechanisms, and explicit tests for where an effect should be absent or reversed.
Maya: And it warns against planting the desired result by moving rows or changing outcomes after generation. If the signal is too weak, change the underlying mechanism and regenerate; don’t quietly edit the evidence until it agrees.
Alex: There’s a strong review loop too: make a small sample, hide the hypotheses and generator logic, then inspect it as a sceptical domain expert. Repeat until the data no longer has obvious synthetic tells, then test at least three seeds.
Maya: That seed check matters. A finding that appears only in the showcased sample is a story about luck, not a robust exercise. And if rare cases need more examples, describe the dataset honestly as an enriched audit sample, not as the whole population.
Alex: The case-study prompt applies the same principle across documents and tables: first simulate what happened, then derive each system’s records from that shared reality. Billing and CRM should disagree for realistic reasons, not because they were made up independently.
Maya: It even asks for harmless anomalies and innocent explanations, so an analyst has to distinguish clues from noise. I like that. Otherwise a training exercise becomes a treasure hunt where every oddity points straight to the answer.

Maya: The files were about 130 kilobytes, within the stated size limits, and the site gave no filename guidance or error message. That’s a good example of why a user’s experience can be blocked by a hidden rule that the interface never explains.

Alex: A very different kind of user-facing detail landed in Liveform. During a workshop, a new question could arrive while someone was halfway down a long form, and the page would jump back to the top.
Maya: The fix tries to preserve the question the person was looking at. It captures a visible question as an anchor, along with the focused field and its text selection. After rendering, it restores that location and focus without forcing a scroll.
Alex: The browser test checks a draft response, a backwards text selection, and a question being added or removed. If the anchored question disappears, the nearest surviving question stays in view. That’s much stronger than just checking that the page still loads.
Maya: The results page gained a presenter control for hidden questions too. Presenters can navigate to a future question, see that it is hidden from the audience, and press Show. Audience browsers pick it up through the normal polling.

Maya: And permission is narrower than simply being allowed to view results. Only an email explicitly listed under `results.auth.allowed_emails` can change visibility; domain access or inherited authentication can still grant viewing, but not write access.

Maya: It’s one of those features where the human consequence is the test. A presenter can stage a sequence without interrupting participants, and a respondent doesn’t lose their place because a question changed somewhere else.
